Transport, Selectivity and Integration of the Trench That Holds the Access Transistor

Preface

Every modern DRAM cell is switched by a transistor that has been pushed underground. The gate of that transistor, the buried word line, sits in a narrow trench cut across silicon and isolation oxide alike, and the shape of that trench decides the cell's on-current, its leakage, and therefore its retention time. Few etches in the fab are so short and so consequential.

This book is organised in four parts and sixteen chapters, with seven appendices, a glossary and an index.

Part I — Foundations (Chapters 1–4): the cell, the process flow, plasma basics, and a computed treatment of neutral transport in a trench.

Part II — Trench Etch Physics (Chapters 5–8): ion delivery and charging, chemistry, the silicon-versus-oxide race that forms the saddle fin, and profile control.

Part III — Process Integration (Chapters 9–12): hard mask and patterning, reactor knobs, the bottom and the fin, and the gate stack and recess that follow.

Part IV — Measurement, Control and Outlook (Chapters 13–16): metrology, modelling, yield and reliability, and the road ahead.

How the numbers were made

Transport numbers in Chapters 4–5 and Appendix B, and the depth histories in Chapter 7, come from a Monte Carlo simulation and rate model (sim/trench_mc.py). The neutral transport code is checked against the exact line-of-sight result for a fully absorbing trench. Everything else quantitative (process windows, dimensions, chemistries) is illustrative, chosen to show orders of magnitude and relationships; none of it describes a specific manufacturer's process. References are listed in Appendix G; readers should check citation details against the originals.

Part I — Foundations

Chapter 1 — Why Bury the Word Line

1.1 The one-transistor, one-capacitor cell

A DRAM bit is a capacitor that holds charge and a transistor that connects it to a bit line when the word line — the transistor's gate — is raised. The capacitor leaks, so every cell must be read and rewritten many times a second; the time a cell can hold its state without refresh is its retention time. Almost every design decision in the array trades on-current (how quickly the capacitor charges and discharges) against off-state leakage (how long it keeps its charge).

For decades the access transistor was a planar MOSFET with a polysilicon gate on top of the silicon. As the cell shrank toward a 6F² footprint (F being the half-pitch of the tightest line), the planar gate length shrank with it, and short-channel leakage became intolerable.

1.2 Recessed channels, then buried gates

The first answer was to recess the channel: etch a short trench into the silicon under the gate so that the current path goes down, around and up. The recess-channel array transistor (RCAT) lengthened the effective channel without growing the cell. A refinement, the saddle fin (or S-RCAT), let the gate wrap the sides of the silicon under the trench as well as its floor, restoring drive current that the longer channel had cost.

The decisive step was to put the entire gate, including its conductor, inside the trench and cap it with dielectric. This is the buried-channel array transistor (BCAT), or simply the buried word line (BWL). The word line runs below the silicon surface, the bit-line contact and storage-node contact land on the surface above it, and the parasitic capacitance between word line and bit line drops sharply. Since roughly the 4x nm generation every major DRAM maker has used some form of buried word line.

1.3 What the trench etch has to deliver

The word-line trench is a line-and-space pattern running across the active area. Along its length it crosses silicon active regions and shallow-trench-isolation (STI) oxide alternately. A single etch step must therefore cut two materials at once and leave them at different depths on purpose:

  • in the active silicon, the floor sets the channel's lower boundary; its depth and roundness set the effective gate length and threshold voltage;
  • in the STI oxide, the floor is deliberately deeper, so that the silicon between neighbouring trenches stands up as a fin that the gate metal wraps — the saddle fin;
  • the sidewalls must be near vertical and smooth, because the gate oxide grown or deposited on them is the transistor's gate dielectric.

Too little fin and the cell is weak; too much and the word line couples to the neighbouring cell's channel (passing-gate or row-hammer effects, Chapter 15). A difference of a few nanometres matters.

1.4 Why this etch is hard

Three themes run through the book:

  1. Two materials, one plasma. Silicon and oxide respond to different parts of the plasma. Chapter 7 shows with a computed model how this makes the oxide overtake the silicon as the trench deepens, which is precisely how the saddle fin forms.
  2. Aspect ratio at small width. Trenches 15–25 nm wide and 120–200 nm deep give aspect ratios of 6–12, enough for transport limits (Chapters 4–5) to set the rate.
  3. Everything that follows depends on it. Gate oxidation, TiN/W fill, metal recess and capping all inherit the trench's shape (Chapter 12).

A note on numbers. Process values quoted in this book are representative and illustrative. The transport and depth-history numbers in Chapters 4, 5 and 7 and Appendix B are computed by sim/trench_mc.py and can be reproduced exactly.

Chapter 2 — The Cell and the Process Flow

2.1 Layout of a 6F² array

In a 6F² layout the active regions are short silicon bars, tilted relative to the word lines, each carrying two transistors that share one bit-line contact in the middle and have a storage-node contact at each end. Two word lines cross each active bar; a third, the passing word line, crosses the STI between bars. Bit lines run perpendicular to the word lines on top.

The word-line trench therefore sees, along its length, an alternating sequence: silicon (an active bar), oxide (the STI between bars), silicon, oxide. The pitch is twice F; at F = 15 nm the trenches are around 15–20 nm wide on a 30–40 nm pitch.

2.2 Where the etch sits in the flow

A representative buried-word-line module runs:

  1. STI formation — isolation trenches etched, lined and filled with oxide, planarised.
  2. Hard-mask stack — oxide or nitride pad, amorphous carbon, and a silicon oxynitride or spin-on cap.
  3. Patterning — lithography (often with self-aligned double or quadruple patterning) and mask open.
  4. Word-line trench etch — the subject of this book; one or more steps that cut silicon and STI oxide.
  5. Clean and gate oxidation — removal of residue and damage; growth (often radical oxidation) of the gate dielectric.
  6. Gate conductor — a TiN liner and tungsten fill, or increasingly a dual work-function stack with a doped-polysilicon or low-work-function upper layer.
  7. Metal recess — etch-back of TiN and W to a controlled depth below the silicon surface.
  8. Cap — silicon nitride fill and planarisation.

Bit-line contacts, bit lines, storage-node contacts and capacitors follow on top.

2.3 Dimensions that matter

The important numbers are relative, so we normalise everything by the trench width w:

2.4 Materials the plasma sees

  • Crystalline silicon in active areas, usually doped; doping affects etch rate modestly in halogen plasmas.
  • STI oxide, often a flowable or high-density-plasma oxide, sometimes with a thermal liner and a nitride liner at the silicon interface. The nitride liner is a notorious source of divots (Chapter 11).
  • Hard mask (amorphous carbon, oxide or nitride) at the top.
  • Redeposited by-products (SiBr_x, SiCl_x, SiO_xBr_y) on the sidewalls, which are both a nuisance and the main profile control lever.

Chapter 3 — Plasma Etch Fundamentals for Trenches

3.1 Ions, neutrals and the sheath

A low-pressure plasma supplies two populations to the wafer. Neutrals — radicals such as Cl, Br, F, O and CF_x — are many, slow and arrive from all directions with a roughly cosine distribution. Ions — Cl₂⁺, Br⁺, Ar⁺ and the like — are few, but are accelerated across the sheath to energies of tens to hundreds of electron-volts and arrive nearly perpendicular to the wafer.

The angular spread of the ions is set by the ratio of their transverse temperature to the sheath voltage. For a collisionless sheath, the characteristic angle is roughly θ ≈ √(kT_i / eV_s); with T_i ≈ 0.05–0.1 eV and V_s ≈ 100 V this gives two to three degrees. Collisions in the sheath at higher pressure broaden it. Chapter 5 uses σ = 2°, 4° and 8° to span the range.

3.2 Ion-neutral synergy

Neither population etches silicon or oxide efficiently on its own. Neutrals adsorb and form a halogenated surface layer; ions supply the energy to break bonds and eject products. The classic Coburn–Winters experiment showed that together they etch an order of magnitude faster than either alone.

A useful model treats the surface as a fraction θ covered by reactant. Neutrals fill it at a rate proportional to their flux Γ_n and the empty fraction; ions empty it at a rate proportional to their flux Γ_i and a yield. At steady state the etch rate becomes

R = 1 / (1/(aΓ_n) + 1/(bΓ_i)),

a series combination of a neutral-supply term and an ion-supply term. When aΓ_n ≪ bΓ_i the etch is neutral-limited; when bΓ_i ≪ aΓ_n it is ion-limited. Chapter 7 builds directly on this form.

3.3 Silicon and oxide behave differently

Silicon in Cl₂/HBr chemistries is etched by halogen radicals with modest ion help; the reaction is substantially neutral-limited at the energies used for trenches. Oxide needs much more ion energy to break Si–O bonds; in halogen chemistry it is strongly ion-limited, and in fluorocarbon chemistry it depends on a polymer film that ions must thin.

This difference is the central fact of word-line trench etch. One plasma meets two surfaces that want different things, and the trench's geometry filters the plasma differently for each.

3.4 Selectivity and loading

Selectivity is the ratio of etch rates of two materials. Three are relevant here: silicon to hard mask, silicon to oxide (or its inverse), and either to the nitride liner. Loading is the dependence of rate on the amount of exposed material; microloading is the dependence on local pattern density; aspect-ratio-dependent etching (ARDE) is the dependence on the feature's depth-to-width ratio. In a dense word-line array, ARDE dominates and is the subject of the next four chapters.

3.5 Passivation

Sidewalls are protected by a thin film of etch products and added gases — SiO_xBr_y from HBr/O₂, or CF_x polymer from fluorocarbons. The film is removed from the floor by ion bombardment but survives on the walls where ion flux is grazing. The balance between deposition and removal sets the taper angle (Chapter 8).

Chapter 4 — Neutral Transport in a Trench

4.1 Free-molecular conditions

At the pressures used for trench etch (a few to tens of millitorr), the mean free path of a neutral is millimetres to centimetres, while the trench is 20 nm wide. The Knudsen number is enormous, so molecules inside the trench never collide with each other — only with the walls. This is free-molecular (Knudsen) flow, and transport becomes a geometry problem.

4.2 The model

The simulation in sim/trench_mc.py treats the trench as infinitely long (the word line is many microns long against a 20 nm width), with walls at x = 0 and x = 1 in units of the width and the floor at depth AR. Each molecule:

  1. enters the top with a uniform position and a cosine angular distribution;
  2. flies straight to the next surface;
  3. at a wall, is lost with probability s (the sticking coefficient, standing for any reaction or recombination that removes it) or re-emitted diffusely with a cosine distribution;
  4. is counted if it reaches the floor, and lost if it flies back out of the top.

Three hundred thousand molecules are traced for each aspect ratio and sticking coefficient.

4.5 What this means for silicon

Silicon etch in halogen chemistry is neutral-limited. Its rate therefore follows these curves closely, and ARDE in silicon is dominated by neutral starvation. Chapter 7 turns these numbers into etch-rate histories.

Part II — Trench Etch Physics

Chapter 5 — Ion Delivery and Charging

5.1 Ions as nearly straight lines

Ions arrive with a narrow angular distribution and, at these energies, do not re-emit usefully after striking a wall: they deposit their energy into the sidewall film, sputter a little of it, and are neutralised. To first order, an ion reaches the floor only if its straight-line path does not hit a wall on the way down.

The simulation launches ions uniformly across the opening with an in-plane angle drawn from a Gaussian of standard deviation σ and counts those that land on the floor. Only the in-plane angle matters for an infinitely long trench; motion along the trench is free.

This is the most important single number in the book. As a trench deepens, its floor becomes progressively ion-rich and neutral-starved. Any material whose etch is neutral-limited slows down faster than any material whose etch is ion-limited.

5.3 Angular spread as a knob

A tighter angular distribution (σ = 2°) keeps 78 % of ions at AR = 8; a broad one (σ = 8°) only 34 %. Raising bias voltage or lowering pressure narrows the distribution. Because the trench is two-dimensional, ions lost on the way down strike the sidewalls, mostly in the top third of the trench. Those wall impacts drive bowing (Chapter 8) and damage the future gate-oxide surface (Chapter 12).

5.4 Charging

Electrons arrive nearly isotropically and are stopped by the sidewalls near the top of the trench; ions reach the floor. On insulating surfaces this separates charge: the upper sidewalls charge negative, the floor positive. In a word-line trench the silicon floor is conducting and drains charge to the substrate, but the STI oxide floor does not. Positive charge on oxide floors deflects late-arriving ions toward the adjacent silicon wall, which is one mechanism for notching at the foot of the saddle fin.

Charging is reduced by pulsed bias (which lets electrons reach deeper during the off phase), by lower aspect ratio at the moment of landing, and by keeping the ion energy distribution narrow. The present model does not include it; its numbers are best read as the uncharged limit.

5.5 Summary

  • Ion delivery falls off slowly with depth; neutral delivery falls off quickly.
  • The floor of a deep trench is therefore relatively ion-rich.
  • Lost ions end up on the sidewalls, where they shape the profile.

Chapter 6 — Chemistry of the Word-Line Trench

6.1 The two jobs of the gas mix

The gas mixture must etch silicon and oxide at controlled relative rates, and it must passivate the sidewalls just enough to keep them straight. No single gas does both well, so mixtures are the rule.

6.2 Halogen chemistries for silicon

HBr is the backbone of silicon trench etch. Bromine is heavy, its silicon bromides are less volatile than chlorides, and the resulting SiBr_x deposits help passivate the walls. HBr etch is highly selective to oxide and photoresist.

Cl₂ etches silicon faster and more isotropically. It is added to raise the rate and to adjust the profile.

O₂ in small fractions oxidises redeposited SiBr_x into a more robust SiO_xBr_y wall film. A few percent O₂ can turn a bowed profile into a tapered one.

NF₃ or SF₆ in small amounts add fluorine, which etches silicon spontaneously and isotropically; it is used to clean the walls of excess film and to round the trench floor.

6.3 Etching the oxide at the same time

Pure HBr/O₂ etches STI oxide slowly, because breaking Si–O bonds requires energetic ions. That is a feature, not a bug: it is this ion-limited oxide etch that lets the oxide catch up with silicon deep in the trench (Chapter 7). When a larger oxide-to-silicon depth difference is wanted, a fluorocarbon step (CF₄, CHF₃ or C₄F₈ with Ar) is added, either before the silicon step to pre-recess the STI, or after it to deepen the oxide only.

6.4 Multi-step recipes

A representative sequence is:

Step Purpose Illustrative chemistry
Breakthrough remove native oxide and residue CF₄ / Ar, short
Main etch silicon and oxide to near-final depth HBr / Cl₂ / O₂
Over-etch / fin define fin height, round floor HBr / O₂, lower bias; or CF_x / Ar for oxide
Soft landing reduce damage low-bias HBr / He

The numbers in Chapter 7 refer to the main etch only.

6.5 By-products and the sticking coefficient

The sticking coefficient s used in Chapter 4 is the probability that a radical is lost at a wall. For Br on a SiO_xBr_y-covered wall s is small (perhaps 0.01–0.1); for O on the same wall it may be larger; for F on bare silicon it is high. Because neutral delivery to the floor depends so strongly on s, changing the wall chemistry changes the floor chemistry. A thick passivation film that consumes radicals also starves the floor.

6.6 Chamber condition

Halogen chemistry deposits on chamber walls, and the wall state changes the plasma's radical densities by tens of percent. Seasoning, wall temperature control and in-situ cleans are as much a part of the recipe as the gas flows; Chapter 15 returns to this as a source of lot-to-lot drift.

Chapter 7 — The Silicon-Oxide Race and the Saddle Fin

7.1 The question

The word-line trench crosses silicon and oxide. At the end of etch, the oxide floor should sit 10–30 nm below the silicon floor so that the silicon forms a fin. How does a single plasma produce that offset, and how sensitive is it?

7.2 A rate model

Combine the synergy expression of Chapter 3 with the floor fluxes of Chapters 4 and 5. Normalise each flux to its value at the top of the trench, so Γ_n(AR) and Γ_i(AR) are the computed floor fractions. Write the rate of each material as

R(AR) = R₀ · (ρ + 1) / (ρ/Γ_n + 1/Γ_i),

where R₀ is the open-area rate and ρ measures how neutral-limited the material is (ρ ≫ 1: neutral-limited; ρ ≪ 1: ion-limited). At the top both fluxes are 1 and R = R₀.

7.4 Consequences

  • Fin height grows with depth. Between t = 10 t₀ and 16 t₀ the fin grows by about 2 nm per t₀, while the silicon floor deepens by only 5–6 nm per t₀. A 5 % error in etch time therefore near t = 15 t₀ changes the fin by about 1.5 nm and the silicon depth by about 4 nm.
  • Fin height depends on width. A narrower trench reaches a given aspect ratio sooner, so at fixed depth it has a taller fin. CD variation across the wafer becomes fin-height variation.
  • The silicon rate at end of etch is a quarter of open-area. By t = 16 t₀ silicon etches at 0.26 R₀. Throughput is spent at the bottom.
  • Endpoint is invisible. No interface is crossed; the etch is timed. Chapter 13 discusses how depth is measured instead.

7.5 Limits of the model

The model ignores charging, profile taper (which changes the effective width with depth), wall-film thickness, and the 3-D structure where silicon and oxide meet along the trench. Real fins are rounded where those effects act. The model's value is in showing the mechanism and the sensitivities, not in predicting a production depth.

Chapter 8 — Profile Control

8.1 What a good profile looks like

The ideal word-line trench has near-vertical sidewalls (88–89°), a slightly rounded floor in silicon, a smooth transition at the fin shoulder, no bow below the mask and no notch at the foot. The sidewall becomes the channel surface, so its roughness directly scatters carriers and its crystal damage degrades gate-oxide quality.

8.2 Taper

Taper — walls that lean inward with depth — arises when passivation deposits faster than ions remove it. More O₂, higher wall-film sticking, or lower bias all increase taper. Mild taper is useful: it narrows the floor, rounds the bottom and makes subsequent fill easier. Excessive taper shortens the effective gate width and pinches the trench before target depth.

8.3 Bowing

Bowing is an outward bulge, usually 20–50 nm below the mask. Chapter 5 showed that ions which miss the floor strike the walls in the upper part of the trench. Ions scattered off the mask facet at grazing angles add to this. Where that wall flux exceeds what the passivation can absorb, the wall etches laterally. Remedies: tighter ion angular distribution, more passivation early, a less faceted hard mask.

8.4 Floor shape

A flat floor with sharp corners concentrates the electric field at the corners of the finished transistor and degrades reliability. A rounded floor is preferred. Rounding comes from a slightly broad ion distribution near the floor, from a short isotropic fluorine step, or from oxidation and strip after etch.

8.5 The fin shoulder

Where the trench crosses from silicon into oxide, the silicon side of the fin is exposed below the silicon floor. Its shape decides how well the gate wraps the fin. Sharp shoulders give field crowding and gate-oxide thinning; very rounded shoulders reduce effective width. The shoulder is shaped by the over-etch step and by subsequent cleans.

8.6 Line-edge roughness and wiggling

Roughness transferred from the mask becomes sidewall roughness. In a 20 nm trench, ±1.5 nm of roughness is 15 % of the width. Line wiggling — low-frequency lateral wander — has less effect on the transistor than on the later contact landing, where a wandering word line narrows the space for the bit-line contact.

Part III — Process Integration

Chapter 9 — Hard Mask and Patterning

9.1 Why a hard mask

Photoresist thin enough to resolve a 20 nm line cannot survive a 150 nm silicon etch. The pattern is therefore transferred first into a hard mask — typically amorphous carbon (a-C) under a silicon oxynitride or oxide cap, sometimes over a thin oxide or nitride pad — and the trench is etched through the hard mask.

9.2 Multiple patterning

At word-line pitches below about 40 nm, single-exposure 193 nm immersion lithography cannot print the lines. Self-aligned double patterning (SADP) forms spacers on a mandrel and uses the spacers as the mask, halving the pitch; self-aligned quadruple patterning (SAQP) repeats the process. EUV lithography can print some of these layers directly. Each approach leaves its signature: SADP and SAQP produce pitch walking, in which alternate spaces differ slightly in width.

Pitch walking matters doubly here. A wider space is a wider trench, which etches faster at a given depth (lower AR) and, by the argument of Chapter 7, leaves a slightly shorter fin. Alternate word lines then differ systematically in threshold voltage.

9.3 Mask open

The cap is opened in fluorocarbon chemistry, the carbon in O₂/N₂ or O₂/COS, and the pad in fluorocarbon again. The carbon open sets the profile that the silicon etch inherits: a tapered or faceted carbon line throws ions onto the trench walls (Chapter 8).

9.4 Mask selectivity budget

With a carbon mask, HBr-based silicon etch has a selectivity of perhaps 10–30. A 150 nm etch then consumes 5–15 nm of carbon in open area, more on the facet. Because silicon slows down with depth (Chapter 7) while mask erosion does not, the effective selectivity over the whole etch is lower than the open-area value — by a factor of more than two in the model of Chapter 7, where silicon averages 0.43 R₀ over a 15 t₀ etch.

9.5 CD control

The final trench CD is set by the mask CD plus any bias introduced by the etch. Bias comes from mask erosion (positive), passivation build-up (negative) and taper. A typical target is zero bias at the top and a controlled taper below.

9.6 Array edge

At the edge of the array the pattern density changes abruptly. Neutral and by-product loading change, and the first few word lines are often dummies. Their trenches etch differently, and the transition is a common place for fin-height and depth excursions.

Chapter 10 — Reactor Knobs: Bias, Pulsing and Temperature

10.1 Tool type

Word-line trench etch runs in inductively coupled (ICP) or similar high-density reactors, where source power sets the plasma density and radical production and a separate bias supply on the wafer sets ion energy. That separation is what makes the Chapter 7 balance tunable.

10.2 Source power

Higher source power raises both ion and radical fluxes, and increases dissociation, shifting the radical mix. Its main effect on the saddle fin is indirect, through the neutral-to-ion ratio at the top.

10.3 Bias power and voltage

Bias raises ion energy, which helps the ion-limited oxide etch more than the neutral-limited silicon etch, and narrows the ion angular distribution. Both push the oxide ahead: more bias, taller fin. Bias also raises sputtering of the mask and sidewall damage, so it is limited.

10.4 Pressure

Lower pressure narrows the ion angular distribution and lowers radical density. In the model, narrowing σ from 4° to 2° keeps more ions on the floor, accelerating the oxide; lower radical density slows silicon. Lower pressure therefore tends to taller fins, at the cost of rate.

10.5 Pulsing

Pulsed source or bias power separates the plasma into on and off phases. During the off phase electrons cool, the sheath collapses, and negative ions and electrons can reach deeper into the trench, partly neutralising charge. Synchronised pulsing of source and bias adds a further degree of freedom: radicals can be produced in one phase and ions delivered in another, which lets the neutral-to-ion ratio at the floor be set nearly independently of geometry.

10.6 Wafer temperature

Sidewall passivation and by-product volatility depend on wafer temperature. Lower temperature favours deposition and taper; higher temperature favours cleaner, more vertical walls and higher sticking for some radicals. Electrostatic-chuck zones let the temperature be adjusted radially to cancel centre-to-edge variation in depth and fin height.

Chapter 11 — The Floor, the Fin and the Liner

11.1 Depth targeting without endpoint

The silicon floor does not stop on anything. Depth is set by time, and is controlled run to run from metrology (Chapter 13). Because silicon slows to a quarter of its open-area rate by the end of etch (Chapter 7), time error translates only weakly into depth error — a useful self-limiting effect.

11.2 The nitride liner and divots

STI trenches are often lined with a thin silicon nitride between the thermal oxide liner and the oxide fill. In the word-line trench the liner appears as a thin vertical sheet between silicon and oxide. If the chemistry etches nitride more slowly than both, a nitride fence remains; if faster, a divot opens. Divots fill with gate metal and create a parasitic, strongly coupled gate at the edge of the active area. They are among the most damaging defects in this module.

11.3 Fin height targeting

Fin height is the difference of two drifting quantities. The sensitivities from Chapter 7 — fin height grows by roughly 2 nm per t₀ late in the etch, and depends on trench width — mean that fin-height control needs both a stable etch time and a stable CD. Two practical schemes:

  • One-step: rely on the transport mechanism of Chapter 7, tuning bias and O₂ to set the offset.
  • Two-step: etch silicon and oxide together to target silicon depth, then deepen the oxide with a selective fluorocarbon step. This decouples the two depths at the cost of an extra step and more charging risk.

11.4 Floor rounding

A rounded silicon floor reduces field crowding and helps the gate oxide grow uniformly. Rounding steps (low-bias, fluorine-containing) also remove the most damaged surface layer. They must not be so isotropic that they widen the trench or undercut the fin.

11.5 Microloading along the trench

The silicon segments of a word-line trench are short (one active area) and alternate with oxide. By-products from silicon etch, mostly SiBr_x, travel along the trench and redeposit on adjacent oxide segments, and vice versa. The result is that floor depths near silicon–oxide transitions differ from those in the middle of each segment, giving the fin its characteristic sloped shoulders.

11.6 Passing word lines

The word line that passes over the STI between two active areas sits beside the end of an active bar. Its oxide floor is deep; the silicon wall it faces is the side of the neighbouring cell's storage-node junction. Too deep an oxide etch brings the passing word line too close to that junction, raising leakage and row-hammer sensitivity (Chapter 15).

Chapter 12 — Gate Stack, Metal Recess and Cap

12.1 Cleaning the trench

After etch, the trench walls carry SiO_xBr_y passivation, implanted halogen and a damaged silicon layer a few nanometres thick. A sequence of dilute HF and SC1-type cleans, sometimes preceded by a sacrificial oxidation, removes them. Each clean also widens the trench and deepens the oxide floor slightly; that consumption must be included in the etch targets.

12.2 Gate oxidation

The gate dielectric is grown on the trench walls, typically by radical (in-situ steam) oxidation, which oxidises (100) and (110) surfaces and the rounded floor at similar rates. Its thickness is around 4–6 nm. Any roughness, residual halogen or crystal damage from the etch shows up as thinning, interface traps or early breakdown.

12.3 Conductor

A thin TiN liner (barrier and work-function setter) and tungsten fill are deposited by CVD or ALD. Tungsten has the lowest resistivity achievable in a 20 nm trench, but it fills by growth from the walls and can leave a seam. Newer stacks use a dual work-function design: a high-work-function metal low in the trench near the channel, and a low-work-function layer (for example doped polysilicon) higher up, adjacent to the drain junctions, to reduce gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL).

12.4 Metal recess

The conductor is etched back to 40–60 nm below the silicon surface. This recess etch must be uniform across the wafer and along the line: too shallow and the gate overlaps the storage-node junction, raising GIDL; too deep and the channel is not fully gated at the top, raising resistance. Recess etch is plasma (SF₆/Cl₂-based for W, Cl₂-based for TiN) or wet, and is a subject in its own right.

12.5 Cap

A silicon nitride cap fills the trench above the recessed metal and is planarised. It insulates the word line from the bit-line and storage-node contacts that will land on the silicon surface beside it.

Part IV — Measurement, Control and Outlook

Chapter 13 — Metrology

13.1 What must be measured

The etch defines five numbers per trench: top CD, bottom CD (or taper), silicon depth, oxide depth and fin height. None is directly visible from above, and two (silicon depth and fin height) are buried under each other.

13.2 Optical critical-dimension metrology

OCD (scatterometry) measures spectra from a periodic target and fits them with a parametric model of the trench. It is fast, non-destructive and runs inline. Its weakness is the model: silicon and oxide segments along the same trench make the structure three-dimensional, and correlated parameters (taper and depth, for instance) are hard to separate. Dedicated targets with silicon-only and oxide-only trench segments help.

13.3 CD-SEM

Top-down CD-SEM measures top CD and roughness directly and can see partially down the trench. It cannot measure depth or fin height.

13.4 X-ray methods

CD-SAXS (critical-dimension small-angle X-ray scattering) passes X-rays through the wafer and measures the full profile of periodic structures with sub-nanometre precision, including taper and depth. Throughput has improved enough for sampling in production.

13.5 Cross-section and TEM

Transmission electron microscopy on lamellae cut across and along the trench is the reference. It shows fin shape, divots and floor rounding unambiguously, but is slow and destructive. It calibrates the inline methods.

13.6 Electrical metrology

Ultimately, the trench is judged by the transistor: threshold voltage, drive current, GIDL and word-line resistance. Electrical test arrives weeks later, so it is used to set targets and to detect drift rather than for run-to-run control.

Chapter 14 — Modelling the Trench Etch

14.1 Levels of model

  • Reactor models compute plasma density, radical composition and ion energy–angle distributions from tool settings.
  • Feature-scale models take those fluxes and compute how a feature's profile evolves.
  • Compact models reduce either to a few fitted parameters for process control.

The model of Chapters 4–7 is a feature-scale model of the simplest useful kind: Monte Carlo transport plus a two-term surface rate law, with the profile reduced to a single depth.

14.2 Monte Carlo transport

Monte Carlo is the natural method for free-molecular transport: trace many molecules, apply the surface rules, count. It handles arbitrary geometries and surface rules without approximation; its cost is noise, which falls as one over the square root of the number of particles. With 300,000 particles the floor fractions in Appendix B carry a standard error below 0.001. Appendix A details the algorithm.

14.3 Profile evolution

Full feature-scale simulators represent the surface by strings, cells or level sets and move each surface element according to its local fluxes. They predict taper, bowing and fin shoulders, which the one-dimensional depth model cannot. They need surface rate laws for each material and each species, fitted from beam experiments or blanket-wafer data.

14.4 Calibration

Calibration is the hard part. The two parameters per material in Chapter 7 — R₀ and ρ — can be extracted from two experiments: an open-area rate, and the rate at one known aspect ratio. Sticking coefficients come from literature or from fits to depth versus width data across a test pattern with several trench widths. Fitting more parameters than the data can constrain is the commonest failure.

14.5 Uses

  • Explaining a sensitivity (why fin height moves with CD).
  • Ranking knobs before running experiments.
  • Building virtual metrology: predicting depth from chamber sensors and incoming CD.
  • Transferring a recipe between chamber types with different ion angular distributions.

14.6 Machine-learning surrogates

Neural-network and Gaussian-process surrogates trained on simulated and measured data are increasingly used for virtual metrology and recipe search. They are most reliable when constrained by physical structure — for example, by predicting ρ and R₀ rather than depth directly.

Chapter 15 — Process Control, Yield and Reliability

15.1 Run-to-run control

Etch time is adjusted lot to lot from post-etch depth metrology and incoming CD. Because fin height grows faster than silicon depth late in the etch (Chapter 7), a controller that holds silicon depth will let fin height drift with CD; a controller that holds fin height will let depth drift. Most fabs control on a combination, weighted by electrical sensitivity.

15.2 Within-wafer uniformity

Centre-to-edge variation in plasma density, wafer temperature and incoming CD produce radial patterns in depth and fin height. Multi-zone gas injection and electrostatic-chuck temperature zones are the main correction tools. Edge rings erode over their life and shift the edge profile, so ring wear is tracked as a control variable.

15.3 Chamber drift

Wall deposits accumulate over a maintenance cycle, changing radical densities and therefore the neutral-limited silicon rate more than the ion-limited oxide rate. The fin height drifts upward as the chamber ages. Seasoning wafers after each clean and periodic in-situ cleans limit the drift.

15.5 Retention and GIDL

Retention failures are dominated by the leakiest cells. Gate-induced drain leakage at the junction where the word-line metal overlaps the storage-node diffusion is set by the metal recess depth (Chapter 12), but its tail is set by trench-wall damage and roughness from the etch.

15.6 Row hammer

Repeatedly activating one word line disturbs cells on adjacent rows. The passing word line beside an active area couples to the storage-node junction of the neighbour; a deeper oxide floor (taller fin) increases that coupling. Row-hammer sensitivity therefore puts an upper limit on fin height that is independent of the drive-current argument for a taller fin.

Chapter 16 — Beyond the Buried Word Line

16.1 Scaling the buried word line

Each generation narrows the trench. At fixed depth, aspect ratio rises, and by the arguments of Chapters 4–7 the silicon slows and the fin grows. Shallower trenches limit this but shorten the effective channel. Thinner gate dielectrics and higher-k materials help preserve control. Word-line resistance rises as the conductor narrows, prompting interest in lower-resistivity metals such as molybdenum and ruthenium.

16.2 4F² and vertical channels

A 4F² cell needs a vertical channel transistor: a silicon pillar with the channel running vertically and the gate wrapped around or beside it, with the bit line buried below. The word-line etch then becomes a trench between pillar rows, or a gate formed by deposition and etch-back on the pillar sides. The high-aspect-ratio pillar etch inherits much of the physics in this book.

16.3 3D DRAM

Several companies have proposed stacking DRAM cells in layers, with horizontal access transistors and capacitors and vertical word or bit lines, borrowing the concepts of 3D NAND. Their etches look more like 3D NAND channel-hole and slit etches than like the trench of this book, with aspect ratios beyond 50 and silicon–silicon-germanium superlattices to cut.

16.4 New etch techniques

Atomic-layer etching separates the neutral and ion steps in time, making the rate self-limiting per cycle and removing the neutral-starvation mechanism of Chapter 7 — at the cost of throughput. Synchronised pulsing offers a middle ground. Cryogenic etch changes the wall chemistry and sticking coefficients dramatically. All three are under evaluation for trench and pillar steps.

16.5 Closing

The buried word-line trench is a short etch that encodes, in a few nanometres of fin height, the competition between two transport regimes. Understanding that competition — neutrals starving, ions persisting — is the key to scaling it, and to the vertical and stacked cells that will replace it.

Appendix A — The Monte Carlo and Rate Model

A.1 Neutral transport

Coordinates are in units of trench width; the walls are x = 0 and x = 1, the opening z = 0 and the floor z = AR. Only the in-plane components (x, z) of a direction matter, because motion along the trench does not change which surface is hit next.

A cosine-law direction about a surface normal is sampled as cos θ = √u, sin θ = √(1−u), with an azimuth φ uniform in [0, 2π); the in-plane tangential component is sin θ cos φ. Entering molecules use the downward normal; wall re-emission uses the inward normal of that wall.

For each molecule the distance to the next wall and to the next end (top or floor) is computed; the nearer one is hit. A floor hit is counted, a top hit is lost, and a wall hit is lost with probability s or re-emitted otherwise. All molecules are processed in vectorised batches.

A.2 Ions

Ions start uniformly across the opening with an in-plane angle θ drawn from N(0, σ²). An ion reaches the floor if x + AR·tan θ lies within [0, 1]; otherwise it strikes a wall and is removed.

A.3 Validation

For s = 1 the neutral floor fraction equals the line-of-sight view factor of the floor from the opening, √(1+AR²) − AR (crossed strings). Table 4.1 compares them. For the ion model the exact result is an error-function integral, which the code reproduces trivially.

A.4 Rate model

R(AR)/R₀ = (ρ + 1)/(ρ/Γ_n + 1/Γ_i), with Γ_n and Γ_i linearly interpolated from the transport table. Depth is integrated by forward Euler with Δt = 0.05 t₀; halving the step changes depths by under 0.1 nm.

Appendix E — Derivations

E.1 Line-of-sight fraction of a 2-D slot

For two-dimensional diffuse radiation between surfaces, the view factor from surface 1 to surface 2 by the crossed-strings rule is (sum of crossed strings − sum of uncrossed strings) / (2 × length of surface 1). For an opening and floor of width 1 separated by AR, the crossed strings each have length √(1+AR²) and the uncrossed ones length AR, so F = √(1+AR²) − AR. At large AR, F ≈ 1/(2AR).

E.2 Synergy rate law

Let θ be the fractional coverage of reactant. Adsorption: dθ/dt = s₀Γ_n(1−θ); removal: Y Γ_iθ. At steady state θ = s₀Γ_n/(s₀Γ_n + YΓ_i), and R ∝ YΓ_iθ = 1/(1/(s₀Γ_n) + 1/(YΓ_i)). Normalising to the open-area fluxes and defining ρ = YΓ_i⁰/(s₀Γ_n⁰) gives R/R₀ = (ρ+1)/(ρ/Γ_n + 1/Γ_i).

E.3 Limits

  • ρ → ∞: R/R₀ → Γ_n, purely neutral-limited.
  • ρ → 0: R/R₀ → Γ_i, purely ion-limited.

E.4 Crossover condition

Silicon (R₀ = 1, ρ_Si) and oxide (R₀ = r < 1, ρ_ox) etch at equal rates when (ρ_Si+1)/(ρ_Si/Γ_n,Si + 1/Γ_i) = r(ρ_ox+1)/(ρ_ox/Γ_n,ox + 1/Γ_i). With the Chapter 7 parameters the rates cross at about AR = 1.3 (a depth of 26 nm); the depths cross later, at AR ≈ 2.7, because oxide must first make up the lead silicon built early.

E.5 Ion floor fraction

For a Gaussian angular spread, the floor fraction is the average over x ∈ [0,1] of P(−x ≤ AR tan θ ≤ 1−x). For small σ this is approximately 1 − AR·σ·√(2/π) when ARσ ≪ 1.

Appendix F — Worked Problems

F.1 Line of sight. A 18 nm trench is 144 nm deep. What fraction of a fully reactive radical reaches the floor directly? AR = 8; W = √65 − 8 = 0.062. About 6 %.

F.2 Neutral starvation. Using Appendix B, by what factor does the floor flux of a radical with s = 0.05 fall between AR = 2 and AR = 8? 0.501 → 0.176, a factor of 2.8.

F.3 Ion persistence. For σ = 4°, by what factor does ion floor flux fall over the same range? 0.889 → 0.570, a factor of 1.6. The floor's ion-to-neutral ratio rises by 2.8/1.6 ≈ 1.8.

F.4 Fin-height sensitivity. From the Chapter 7 table, estimate the change in fin height if the etch stops at 14 t₀ instead of 15 t₀. 20.3 nm versus 22.3 nm: 2 nm shorter. Silicon depth changes by 5.4 nm.

F.5 Real time. With w = 20 nm and an open-area silicon rate of 240 nm/min, how long is 15 t₀? t₀ = 20/240 min = 5 s; 15 t₀ = 75 s.

F.6 Pitch walking. Two alternate trenches are 19 nm and 21 nm wide. At equal etch time, which has the taller fin, and why? The narrower one: it reaches a given aspect ratio at a shallower depth, so its silicon slows earlier while its oxide keeps going (Chapter 7).

F.7 Effective selectivity. Open-area silicon-to-carbon selectivity is 20. The silicon averages 0.43 R₀ over the etch, while carbon erodes at a constant rate. What is the effective selectivity? 20 × 0.43 ≈ 8.6.
